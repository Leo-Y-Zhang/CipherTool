The rain had been falling on Kettleford since before dawn, and by the time Owen Pike pushed open the door of the harbour office he had brought most of it in with him on his shoulders. Marla Denby did not look up from the ledger. She turned a page, ran her finger down a column of figures, and made a small mark in pencil at the bottom.

"You can shut that door," she said. "The wind takes the papers off the shelf and I am the one who picks them up."

"Good morning to you as well," Owen answered, and closed it with his heel. "I have come about the letter."

"I assumed you had. Nobody walks up that hill in this weather to tell me they are happy."

"Then you already know what I am going to say."

"I know roughly," she said. "I would rather hear it in your own words, because the version I have in my head is probably worse than the real one, and I would like to be pleasantly surprised for once."

Owen took off his cap and turned it slowly in both hands. He was a broad man with a way of standing that made small rooms feel smaller, and he had learned over many years that the trick with Marla was never to raise his voice first. "The fee has gone up by a third," he said. "Not a little. A third. I have had the same berth since my father had it, and in all that time it has gone up by a few pounds here and there, the way things do. Now it goes up by a third in one letter, and the letter does not explain itself."

"The letter explains itself in the second paragraph."

"The second paragraph says the increase reflects necessary works to the north wall. That is not an explanation, Marla. That is a sentence somebody wrote so that they would not have to have this conversation."

Marla closed the ledger and set her pencil across it. "That is fair," she said. "I did write it, and I did write it that way, and I will tell you honestly that I wrote it at half past nine at night when I had eleven more of them to do. So let us have the conversation instead. The north wall is failing. Not the top of it, which everybody looks at, but the bottom of it, which nobody does. The surveyor came in March and again in June, and the second report was a good deal less cheerful than the first."

"How much less cheerful?"

"He used the word urgent. He does not strike me as a man who uses that word for the fun of it."

Owen sat down on the bench under the window without being asked. "And the council will not pay?"

"The council will pay for some of it. That is what the meeting on Thursday is about. But they will only pay for some of it, and they have made it clear that they expect the harbour to find the rest from its own income, and the harbour's own income is thirty one boats and a car park."

"Thirty boats," Owen said. "Hallam took his out in April and he is not bringing it back."

"Then it is thirty, which makes my case rather than yours." She almost smiled. "You see the shape of the problem. If the wall goes, the pontoons go with it, and then there is no berth to argue about the price of."

"I understand the shape of the problem," Owen said. "What I do not understand is why the shape of the problem should sit on my shoulders in a single winter. You have put the whole increase into one year. Spread it over three and I will not say another word about it."

Marla leaned back. Outside, the rain went across the window in a long slanting sheet, and somewhere down in the basin a halyard was slapping against a mast in a way that would drive a person mad by evening. "Say that again," she said.

"Spread it over three years. A ninth this year, a ninth next year, a ninth the year after. You get your money. I get to keep my boat in the water and still buy coal."

"Say it again with the word compound in it, because that is what you are really asking for, and I would like us both to be honest about the arithmetic."

Owen laughed, which surprised them both. "You are a hard woman."

"I am an accurate one. There is a difference, although I accept that from the outside they can look similar." She pulled a fresh sheet of paper towards her. "The trouble with three years is that the builder does not want to be paid in three years. He wants to be paid when the scaffolding comes down, and he is quite right to want that, because the last man who did work for this harbour waited fourteen months and told everybody in the county about it."

"Then borrow it."

"Against what? The harbour owns a wall that is falling down and a hut with a leak in the roof."

"Against the fees," Owen said. "You have thirty berths and a waiting list. Any bank will look at that."

Marla was quiet for a moment. She wrote something at the top of the paper and underlined it twice. "That is not a stupid idea," she said at last. "I will tell you what is wrong with it, and then I will tell you what is right with it, and you can decide whether you have improved my morning or ruined it. What is wrong with it is that the waiting list is not a promise. Half of the people on it put their names down four years ago and would not answer the telephone if I rang. What is right with it is that I have never actually asked a bank, and I have been assuming the answer for two years without checking, which is the sort of thing I would tell somebody else off for."

"So you will ask."

"I will ask on Monday. In the meantime, do not go telling the whole quay that the increase is cancelled, because it is not, and if you do I shall have twelve people in here by Friday and no wall by spring."

"I shall say you are considering it."

"You shall say nothing at all, Owen, and I shall love you for it."

He stood, and put his cap back on, and stopped with his hand on the latch. "One more thing," he said. "Whatever happens, do not let them close the slipway while the work is on. Six of us come out of the water in November. If we cannot use the slip we have to go round to Marridge, and Marridge charges by the hour and the tide there is wrong for a keel like mine."

"I had not thought about the slipway," Marla admitted.

"I know you had not. That is why I said it."

After he had gone she sat for a while with the paper in front of her and wrote three lines on it: ask the bank, keep the slip open, tell people the truth before they hear a rumour. Then she looked at the clock, said something under her breath, and went to find a chair that did not wobble, because the girl coming at eleven was going to have to sit on it.

Ellis Traynor arrived at seven minutes to eleven with her hair flattened by the weather and a folder held inside her coat to keep it dry. She was nineteen. She had the particular stillness of somebody who has decided in advance not to fidget, and it lasted about four minutes.

"You found it," Marla said.

"I asked in the bakery. The woman said to walk up until I ran out of road, and then keep going."

"That is the best description of this office anybody has ever given. Sit down. Are you cold?"

"A bit."

"There is a heater under the desk and it works if you kick it. Do not be polite about it. Right. I am going to tell you what the job is, and then I am going to ask you some questions, and then you can ask me whatever you like, and I would rather you asked something difficult than something safe."

"All right," Ellis said.

"The job is assistant harbour master, which sounds grand. It is not grand. Three days a week to start, possibly five by summer if the money holds. You will take fees, answer the radio, keep the visitor book, chase people who owe us money, and in August you will spend a great deal of time explaining to men in expensive jackets why they cannot moor where they have decided to moor. Does any of that worry you?"

"The men in jackets do not worry me," Ellis said. "The radio does."

"Good answer. Why?"

"Because I have never used one, and because if I get it wrong on a radio, everybody hears it."

Marla nodded slowly. "That is exactly right, and it is the reason most people are bad at it. They are so frightened of sounding foolish that they say too much. I would rather you said four words than forty. Have you been on the water at all?"

"Some. My uncle has a small boat at Corrin. I have crewed for him since I was eleven, mostly in the summer, mostly badly at the start."

"What does badly mean?"

Ellis thought about it. "It means I did what he told me without understanding why, so when the wind changed I did the same thing again and it was the wrong thing. He shouted. Then he made me sit and explain the tide to him out loud, and after that I was better."

"Did you mind the shouting?"

"I minded it at the time. I do not mind it now."

"You may hear some here," Marla said. "Not from me, as it happens. I have found that if you never raise your voice, the one time you do it, people go very quiet. But you will hear it from others, usually from someone who has just made a mistake in front of his friends and needs it to be your fault. Can you stand still while a grown man tells you that you have ruined his holiday?"

"Yes."

"You answered that quickly."

"I work in the chemist on Saturdays," Ellis said. "A woman told me last month that I had personally destroyed her daughter's wedding because we did not have a particular shade of something in stock. I did not laugh, and I did not cry, and I found her the nearest thing we had."

"Then we shall get on." Marla pushed the folder back across the desk without opening it. "I do not need to read that. I will read it later out of politeness. Tell me instead what you would do in this situation, and take your time, because there is no clever trick in it. It is half past six in the evening in the last week of August. The visitor pontoon is full. A boat comes in on the radio and says he has a family aboard, he is low on fuel, and he intends to come alongside whether or not there is space. What do you say to him?"

Ellis was quiet for longer than most people would have allowed themselves. "I would ask him how much fuel he actually has," she said, "because low on fuel can mean two very different things, and I would want to know which one it is before I decided anything."

"He says he has about twenty minutes."

"Then I would stop worrying about the pontoon. I would tell him where the fuel berth is and whether he can reach it, and if he cannot, I would ask if there is anywhere he can anchor safely and wait, and I would tell you at once."

"And if I am not here?"

"Then I would ring you, and while the telephone was ringing I would be looking at the chart."

"And if he is lying about the fuel because he wants a place at the pontoon?"

"He probably is," Ellis said. "But if I treat him as a liar and he is not, then the mistake is a great deal worse than being made a fool of."

Marla put both hands flat on the desk. "That is the answer I was hoping for and I am pleased with you for getting to it slowly rather than quickly. I will tell you the part that only comes from doing it. Nine times out of ten he is exaggerating, and the way you handle that is not to accuse him. You simply become extremely helpful and extremely specific. You tell him the exact charge for the fuel berth, and you tell him that you will need his details, and you ask what his engine takes. A man who is inventing a problem loses interest in it the moment it involves paperwork."

Ellis laughed. "That is unkind."

"It is unkind and it works, and the two are not the same thing. Now. Ask me something."

"Why did the last person leave?"

"That is the difficult question," Marla said. "Good. She left because I was not fair to her. I gave her all the work I did not enjoy, and none of the work that would have taught her anything, and when she said so I told her she was not ready. She was ready. She is now running a much bigger harbour along the coast and she sends me a card at Christmas, which I choose to believe is friendliness rather than revenge."

"You did not have to tell me that."

"No. But you would have found out within a fortnight, because everybody here talks, and it is better that you hear it from me while I still look like an honest woman." She stood and pulled a battered book from the shelf. "Come here. If you take the job you will need this before anything else."

Ellis came and stood beside her. The book was thick, soft at the corners, and full of columns of numbers.

"This is the tide table for the year," Marla said. "Not the little one they sell in the shop. That one is wrong for us by about twenty minutes and every summer somebody puts a boat on the mud believing it. This is the real one. Now, the rule I want you to learn today, and only this one, is that the number in this column is the height of the water above chart datum, and chart datum is not the sea bed. People assume it is. It is not. It is a level chosen so that the water hardly ever goes below it. So when a man tells you he draws one and a half metres and the table says one point eight, you do not say yes, you say wait."

"Why wait?"

"Because the depth on the chart is added to the number in the table, and because the wind can take half a metre off the whole harbour for two days if it sits in the north east. Numbers on paper are a promise about an ordinary day. Your job is to notice when the day is not ordinary."

"How do I know when it is not?"

"You look at the wall," Marla said. "There is a green line of weed on the north wall and it is at the same level all the way along. If the water is well under that line at high tide, something is going on, and you do not need any book to tell you." She closed the tide table. "Now, the money. It is not much. It is one hundred and forty a week to start, and there is a bicycle that belongs to the harbour which you may use and which you will probably have to repair. If you want the job, it is yours from the first of next month."

"I want it," Ellis said.

"Do not say yes in this room. Go home, tell whoever you tell, and telephone me tomorrow. I would rather you spent one night thinking about the winter than three months regretting the summer."

The council officer came on Thursday, as promised, and he came in a car that was far too clean for the road that led to the harbour. Desmond Vail was a careful man in his fifties who had a habit of repeating the last three words of a sentence before answering it, which Marla found maddening and had decided, before he arrived, that she would not mention.

"Necessary structural works," he said, sitting down. "Yes. I have read the report. I have read both reports, in fact, and I would say that the second one is written in stronger language than the first."

"It is written in stronger language because the wall is in a worse state," Marla said. "The engineer did not become more dramatic. The wall became more urgent."

"More urgent. Quite. Now, the difficulty on our side is the timing rather than the sum. The capital budget for this year is committed. What I can offer you, and I want to be clear that this is not a promise until the committee meets in October, is a contribution of some sixty per cent, paid in the next financial year, against work that begins this winter."

"So you want me to pay for it now and be reimbursed later."

"I want the work to begin before it becomes an emergency, and I am telling you what I can realistically get through a committee. Those are two different sentences, and I would ask you to hear both of them."

Marla considered him. "That is the most useful thing anybody from the council has said to me in four years," she said. "All right. Then let us talk about the forty per cent and the gap in between, because I have been to the bank."

"You have been to the bank."

"On Monday. They will lend against the berth income if the lease on the harbour runs for longer than the loan. The lease has eleven years left. The loan would be seven. So on that point there is no obstacle, and I have the letter here to prove it, which I am showing you now rather than waving at you in October."

Vail read the letter properly, which raised him a little further in her estimation. "This is a good deal more organised than I expected," he said.

"You expected me to come with a grievance."

"I expected you to come with a grievance, yes, and I say that without any disrespect. Most people do, and most of them are entitled to."

"I have a grievance as well," Marla said. "I will come to it. But I would rather have the wall than the argument, so let us finish the wall first. Sixty per cent from you in the next financial year. Forty per cent from a loan against fees, which the harbour will service out of an increase to the berth holders. The increase will be spread across three years rather than one, because otherwise I will lose four or five boats this winter and the income the bank is lending against will start shrinking on the day the money arrives."

"Three years is slower than I would like."

"Three years is the only version of this that survives contact with the people who actually pay," she said. "I can show you the arithmetic. I have done it twice, once hoping to be wrong."

"And your grievance?"

"The slipway. There is a proposal in the works schedule to close it from October to March for access. If you close it for that whole period, six boats cannot come out of the water here and will have to go to Marridge, and at least two of those owners will simply not come back, because once a man has moved his boat he discovers that the new place has a better car park."

Vail wrote something down. "What would you need?"

"Two weekends. Not the whole winter. The first weekend in November and the third. If the contractor knows now, he can plan around it. If he finds out in October he will charge me for it, and he will be within his rights."

"Two weekends," Vail said. "I think that is deliverable. I shall put it in the specification rather than in a covering note, because a covering note is a wish and a specification is a term."

"Then we understand each other."

"I believe we do. May I say one thing before I go, and you may take it however you wish. You have made this very easy to support. The reason committees say no is not usually that they disagree. It is that the person asking has not told them what happens if they say yes, and so the safest thing is to defer. You have told me exactly what happens. I shall be able to repeat it without notes, which is worth more than you would think."

When he had gone, Marla put the kettle on and stood at the window with her arms folded, watching the tide come up the mud towards the green line on the wall. The telephone rang at four.

"It is Ellis. You said to ring."

"I did. And?"

"I still want it."

"Then it is yours," Marla said. "Come on Monday at eight, not eleven, and wear boots you do not mind ruining. We shall start with the radio, because you told me it frightened you, and I have never yet seen a good reason to leave the worst thing until last."

"What should I read before then?"

"Nothing," Marla said. "Sleep. There is a great deal to learn here, and almost none of it is in a book, and the ones who do best are the ones who arrive rested and ask why about four hundred times in the first month. Can you manage four hundred?"

"I can probably manage more than that," Ellis said.

"That," said Marla, "is what I was afraid of, and it is also why I am pleased. Eight o'clock. Bring a flask. The heater lies."
