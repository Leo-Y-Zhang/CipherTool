The town of Vellanport stands on a grey shelf of rock where two small rivers come down to the sea, and for the better part of four hundred years it has smelled of tar, salt and wet rope. Nobody planned it. The first houses were thrown up by fishermen who wanted somewhere dry to mend their nets, and the streets that grew between them still follow the lines those men walked when they carried their gear up from the beach. There is no straight road in the old quarter. A traveller coming down from the hills sees the whole place at once, laid out like a spill of pale stones, with the harbour wall curving out to the east and the lighthouse at the end of it looking, from a distance, like a chalk mark on the water. In winter the wind comes off the sea without anything to slow it, and the older cottages are built low and thick walled for that reason, with windows no bigger than a man could put his shoulders through. It is a hard town to love at first sight. Those who stay tend to stay for good.

Aurelia Mensk was born in one of those low cottages in the spring of seventeen hundred and nine, the fourth child and the second daughter of a cooper who made barrels for the salted fish trade. Her father was a careful man with poor eyes, and by the time she was nine she had taken over the fine work of fitting the hoops, because she could see the join and he could not. This is the small accident on which a great deal later turned. A child who spends her days measuring wood learns without being taught that a difference too small to see may still be a difference that matters. Her mother wanted her apprenticed to a seamstress. Her father, who was in his quiet way an ambitious man, wrote instead to an instrument maker in the county town, a Mister Halbert Coyne, who mended the compasses and quadrants used by the fishing fleet and by the coastal traders. Coyne had no children of his own and a workshop full of unfinished orders. He agreed to take her for three years. She stayed for eleven.

What Coyne taught her was patience, and what she taught herself was arithmetic. The workshop kept a shelf of navigation tables, thumbed and stained, and in the evenings she worked through them until she understood not merely how a position was found but why the method failed as often as it did. The problem was not a mystery to anyone at sea. A ship could establish how far north or south she lay by measuring the height of the sun at noon, and this was reliable enough that a competent master would trust his life to it. Establishing how far east or west she lay was another matter entirely. It required knowing, at the moment of the local noon, what the hour was at some fixed place ashore, and the only instruments that could carry the hour across an ocean were clocks, and clocks at that date could not be trusted on a moving deck. Heat swelled their parts. Cold shrank them. Damp rusted them. The roll of a ship interfered with the swing of a pendulum, and a pendulum that does not swing evenly keeps no time at all.

Mensk did not solve this problem alone, and it is one of the persistent untruths of popular history that she did. What she made, between about seventeen hundred and thirty six and seventeen hundred and forty four, was a sequence of four machines in which the errors were made to cancel one another out. Her insight, if a single one can be named, was that a clock at sea should be designed not to avoid disturbance but to be indifferent to it. Where other makers had tried to shield the mechanism from the motion of the vessel, she built a balance that used two springs of different metals, arranged so that when heat lengthened one it also stiffened the other. The two faults, meeting, largely destroyed each other. The fourth machine, which survives and can still be wound, is about the size of a dinner plate. It lost eleven seconds on a voyage of fifty three days. That is a sentence worth reading twice, because in the language of the trade it meant that a ship could know her longitude to within a few miles after nearly two months out of sight of land.

The consequences reached a long way beyond the workshop. Vellanport had for generations sent its boats out along a coastal route that was safe only because it was slow, hugging the shore where the water was known. Insurance rates in the port were among the highest anywhere on that coast, and the underwriters were quite right to set them high, since roughly one voyage in fourteen ended with a total loss. Within twenty years of the first sea trials, the same underwriters were writing policies at less than half the old rate, and the traffic through the harbour had trebled. A new quay was cut into the rock on the western side. Warehouses went up behind it, four storeys of yellow brick with hoists on the top floor, and the men who built them were paid out of profits that a generation earlier would not have existed. None of this was Mensk's intention. She wanted an accurate clock. What she got, by way of a hundred decisions made by people she never met, was a town with a different shape.

It was on the strength of the new reliability at sea that the northern voyages began. The Sarrow Ice, as the charts of the period call it, is a body of pack ice lying off a stretch of tundra some nine hundred miles beyond the last inhabited islands, and until the middle of the century it was believed, on the evidence of a single disputed sighting, to conceal a navigable channel running west. The belief was attractive because it was profitable. A channel would have cut perhaps four thousand miles from the passage to the eastern markets, and the merchants who put up the money for the first attempt did so on a straightforward calculation of what such a saving would be worth. They were not adventurers. They were men with ledgers, and the ledgers said that a one in five chance of success justified the outlay.

The ship they chose was a converted whaler of two hundred and ninety tons, renamed the Corncrake, and her master was a taciturn Vellanport man called Edrick Sale who had spent nineteen years in the northern fishery and had buried two brothers there. Sale insisted on three things before he would sign: a double hull, a full year of provisions rather than the eight months the owners proposed, and a surgeon who had actually served at sea. He got the first two. The surgeon they sent him, a young man named Peter Ravell, had never been further from home than the county fair, but he turned out to be the most useful person aboard, for reasons that had nothing to do with surgery. The Corncrake sailed on the ninth of June, seventeen hundred and sixty one, with a crew of forty one, watched from the harbour wall by a crowd that the parish records describe as the largest gathering in the town's memory.

For eleven weeks the voyage went well. They made the last of the inhabited islands in good order, watered there, took on a quantity of dried fish, and pushed north into a sea that Ravell described in his journal as being the colour of poured lead and quite silent, so that the working of the ship's timbers could be heard from one end of the deck to the other. On the twenty second of August they met the ice. It was not the wall that the crew had been told to expect but a scatter of floes, widely spaced, with clear lanes between them, and Sale took the ship through at a walking pace with a man in the bows calling the water. They went on in this fashion for nine days and covered rather more than two hundred miles. Then the wind swung round to the north west and held there, and the scattered floes came together like a closing hand.

The Corncrake was frozen in on the third of September and did not float free again for nine months and eleven days. What that meant in practice was a winter of darkness spent in a wooden box, gripped by an ice field that groaned all night and occasionally, without warning, lifted the whole ship several feet and let her down again. Sale did the sensible things. He banked the hull with snow for insulation, rationed the coal from the first week rather than the last, and set every man a fixed duty for every day, on the sound principle that idleness kills faster than cold. Ravell did the thing that saved them. Having read, before sailing, an account of the sickness that afflicted long voyages, he had insisted that a portion of the ship's cargo be given over to barrels of preserved berries and to a quantity of dried herbs which the islanders used in tea. He made every member of the crew drink a measure of the resulting infusion each morning, and he made himself thoroughly unpopular by standing over them while they did it. Four men died that winter, three of an accident on the ice and one of a fever that nobody could name. Not one died of the scurvy. On voyages of comparable length in the same decade, losses of a third of the crew were normal.

They came home in the autumn of seventeen hundred and sixty two, having proved that the channel did not exist, or at any rate that it could not be found where it had been said to be. In the strict terms of the ledgers, the voyage was a failure and the investors lost a great deal of money. In every other respect it changed the northern trade. Ravell's journal was printed in full three years later, at his own expense and without illustrations, and it was read by every serious northern master within a decade. His account of the diet, in particular, was copied into the standing orders of two large trading companies. It is an ordinary irony of the period that the expedition is remembered for a discovery about food, made by a surgeon who had never before been to sea, on a voyage whose entire purpose was geographical.

While the Corncrake was still in the ice, the long quarrel between the Kesh Republic and the Duchy of Marran was moving from argument to war. The immediate cause was a dispute over the tolls charged on the river traffic through the Marran lowlands, but the tolls were only the surface of it. Underneath lay a change that had been working for thirty years. The Kesh had grown rich on textiles, and their looms depended on wool that came almost entirely down that river, so that a duty which had been a nuisance in seventeen hundred and thirty had become, by seventeen hundred and sixty, a hand around the throat of the whole Kesh economy. The Duke's ministers understood this perfectly. They raised the duty in the spring of seventeen hundred and sixty one by a little over a third, calculating that the Kesh would pay because they had no alternative. It was a reasonable calculation and it was wrong.

The Kesh had an alternative, and it was war. Their army was small but it was paid on time, which was rarer at that date than it sounds, and it had spent a decade drilling under an unglamorous officer named Ansel Torvig who had made himself an expert in the movement of supplies. Torvig's contribution to the art of war was not tactical. He simply understood that an army which arrives fed and dry beats an army twice its size that arrives hungry, and he organised his campaigns backwards from the question of what could be carried and how far. When the Kesh crossed the frontier in October of seventeen hundred and sixty one, they did so along the line of the metalled roads and never got more than two days' march from a river depot. The Duchy, whose forces were larger and better mounted, spent the first six weeks of the war marching about the countryside looking for an enemy who was always somewhere else, and eating the crops of their own farmers as they went.

The war was decided at Oldwater on a cold morning in February. The town sits at the only stone bridge across the Sarn for some forty miles in either direction, and both commanders knew that whoever held it controlled the campaign. The Duchy got there first, with about eleven thousand men, and took up a strong position on the high ground behind the town. Torvig arrived two days later with rather fewer than eight thousand and did not attack. Instead he sent a third of his force upstream by night, to a ford that local carters used in dry weather and which appeared on none of the Duchy's maps, and by the middle of the following morning he had a substantial body of infantry on the same bank as the enemy and behind their left. The Duchy commander, a capable but conventional soldier named Rothe, turned to face the new threat and in doing so opened the road to the bridge. The battle that followed lasted less than four hours. Casualties were heavy on both sides, and the parish church at Oldwater still holds the register in which the curate wrote, in a shaking hand, the names of the eighty one townspeople who were killed in the streets when the fighting came among the houses.

The Treaty of Fenlow, signed in the July of seventeen hundred and sixty three, was a short document of nine articles and it is chiefly interesting for what it failed to anticipate. The tolls were abolished, which was what the Kesh had gone to war for. The frontier was moved south by some fifteen miles, which was what they took because they could. Two of the articles dealt with the exchange of prisoners and one with the treatment of merchants, and all three were honoured. The seventh article, which nobody at the table thought important, guaranteed to the inhabitants of the transferred districts the right to be tried under their own customary law rather than the law of the Republic. It was inserted at the request of a Marran negotiator who wanted something to show his master, and the Kesh conceded it without argument because they assumed the distinction would fade within a generation.

It did not fade. Over the following eighty years the transferred districts became, in effect, a separate jurisdiction inside the Republic, with their own courts, their own rules of inheritance, and eventually their own quiet sense of being a people apart. The customary law recognised forms of common ownership of pasture which Kesh law did not, and this had the practical effect that the great enclosures which reshaped the rest of the Republic in the early part of the following century simply did not happen there. Travellers of the eighteen forties describe the districts as backward, and by the measures such travellers used they were. They were also, by the same accounts, better fed, less crowded and markedly less prone to the outbreaks of disorder that troubled the manufacturing towns. A clause written to spare one man's embarrassment at a conference table shaped the daily life of half a million people for a century. Historians of the treaty tend to spend their pages on the tolls. The tolls were settled and forgotten. The seventh article is still being argued about in the courts.

The peace brought money, and the money went into canals. The Republic had rivers but they ran the wrong way, north to south, while the coal lay in the east and the mills that needed it stood in the west. Between seventeen hundred and seventy and eighteen hundred and ten something over nine hundred miles of navigable cut was dug across the country, most of it by hand, by gangs of men who worked with barrows and picks and were paid by the yard. The engineer whose name is most attached to this work is Thomas Ardley, a millwright's son from the western hills who could not, on the evidence of his surviving letters, spell with any consistency, and who nonetheless possessed a sense for the fall of ground that his rivals frankly envied. Ardley walked his routes. He is said to have covered the line of the Great Northern Cut eleven times on foot before a spade went into the earth, and the survey he produced at the end of it required only two significant alterations during eight years of construction.

His masterpiece was the crossing of the Fennow marsh, a stretch of nineteen miles where the ground was so soft that the first attempt sank a completed embankment out of sight in a single winter. Ardley's solution was to stop fighting the marsh. He laid a raft of brushwood and timber, floating rather than founded, and built the channel on top of it, accepting that the whole structure would settle and designing it so that it could settle evenly. Engineers of the following century, who preferred stone and certainty, thought the method crude. It is still carrying water. The cost of moving a ton of coal from the eastern pits to the western mills fell, between seventeen hundred and seventy five and eighteen hundred and five, from about two shillings and sixpence to less than fivepence, and it is not an exaggeration to say that every chimney raised in the west during those thirty years stood on the strength of that reduction.

Nothing about this progress was gentle. The digging killed men steadily and unremarkably, by drowning, by falls of earth, and by the fevers that hung over the wet ground in late summer. It was in the third of these that Doctor Marta Hallowell made her reputation. She had come to the marsh country as an assistant to her uncle, who held the contract to attend the navigators on the Fennow works, and she kept, from the beginning, a book of every case she saw. Where the men slept. Where they drew their water. Which huts were affected and which were not. Her uncle believed, as most of the profession then believed, that the fever rose from the marsh air itself, and the standard remedy was to shut the windows at night. Hallowell noticed that the huts with the worst records were not the ones nearest the water but the ones furthest from the clean spring, and that the men who drank from the cut itself, because it was nearer, fell sick at something like four times the rate of the rest.

She published her figures in eighteen hundred and two in a pamphlet of thirty pages, and she was ignored, politely, for nineteen years. This is worth dwelling on, because the modern habit is to imagine that good evidence carries the day. It does not, or not quickly. Hallowell's difficulty was that she could offer no mechanism. She could show that the pattern held, over some two thousand cases, but she could not say why water should carry a fever, and in the absence of a why her numbers looked to her contemporaries like a coincidence dressed up. What eventually changed matters was not an argument but an accident. In the summer of eighteen hundred and twenty one a contractor on an unrelated works, wanting to save the cost of carting, moved his men's water supply from a spring to a cut, and the outbreak that followed was so sudden and so exactly confined to his gangs that it could not be explained away. Hallowell was then sixty three. She lived long enough to see her pamphlet reprinted, and to be told by a younger man at a public meeting that her conclusions had lately been established.

She died in eighteen hundred and thirty four and is buried at Oldwater, a few streets from the register of the dead of the battle. The two stories are not usually told together, and there is no reason in strict logic why they should be, but they belong to the same country and very nearly to the same families. The grandsons of men who fought at the bridge dug the Fennow cut. The clocks that Aurelia Mensk built in a workshop above a chandler's shop made possible the voyages that made possible the trade that paid the taxes that funded the war. History as it is usually written cuts these threads apart into subjects, and the subjects are convenient, but the people who lived through it experienced only one thing happening, all at once, mostly without explanation.

We know what we know about all of this through a thin and accidental set of survivals. The Vellanport customs records exist because a clerk in eighteen hundred and sixty, told to clear a damp cellar, could not bring himself to burn them and carried them home instead. The Corncrake journals exist because Ravell printed them. Mensk's workshop notes do not exist at all, having gone into the fire with everything else when the building burned in the winter of seventeen hundred and eighty one, and what we have in their place is a bundle of letters to her sister which discuss the work only in passing, between remarks about the weather and a persistent complaint about the price of bread. The historian's craft consists very largely in reading such letters carefully and resisting the temptation to fill the gaps with what ought to have been there. The gaps are enormous. That is the honest position, and it is a better one than the confident tale, because a story built on what actually survives can be corrected, while a story built on what feels right cannot be corrected at all.
