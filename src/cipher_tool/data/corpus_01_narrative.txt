The rain began an hour after dark, and by the time Mira Salt reached the ridge road it had settled into the steady, patient sort of rain that means to keep going all night. She pulled the collar of her coat higher and let the horse pick its own pace. There was no sense in hurrying on wet stone. The road ran along the spine of the hills for eleven miles before it dropped towards the valley, and every one of those miles was loose shale and old ruts, and a horse that broke a leg up here would be the end of the whole errand.

She carried a leather satchel under her coat, buckled twice and sealed with a plug of grey wax. She had not looked inside it. That was the first rule of the trade, and the only one she had never once been tempted to break, because a courier who looked was a courier who could be questioned, and a courier who could be questioned was worth killing. What she knew was the weight of the thing, which was not much, and the address on the outer flap, which was a house on the far side of the county called Ardwen, and the fact that a man in a wet yard in Bellmarch had given her twice the usual fee without arguing about it.

The horse was a stubborn brown mare called Tuppence who hated bridges and loved apples, and between those two facts Mira had built four years of working understanding. They had crossed the county nine times together. Tuppence knew the ridge road better than Mira did, and when the mare stopped dead in the middle of it and put her ears flat, Mira stopped too and listened.

Below and behind them, faint under the hiss of the rain, came the sound of hooves. Not one horse. Three, perhaps four, moving fast and not caring who heard. Mira sat very still and counted the beats and then turned Tuppence off the road and into the gorse, where a shepherd's track went down the eastern face of the hill in a series of tight, ugly switchbacks. She had used it twice before, both times in daylight, and both times she had promised herself she would never use it in the dark.

The descent took the better part of an hour. Water ran down the track in a thin sheet and made every stone into a small trap. Twice the mare slid and caught herself, and twice Mira felt her own heart go somewhere up behind her teeth. Thorns dragged along her boots. Somewhere above her a bird she could not name kept making a noise like a rusty gate. When they finally came out onto the flat, the ground was soft and wet and gave under the hooves with a sucking noise, and Mira let out a breath she had been holding for a long time.

She rode east through the water meadows with the rain on her left cheek. The river Lann lay somewhere out ahead in the dark, wide and brown and swollen with three days of weather, and beyond the river was the low country and the road to Ardwen. There was a bridge at Coldbrook and a ferry at Hurst, and the bridge was nearer, and the bridge was also exactly where anybody who wanted to find her would sit down and wait. So she went to the ferry.

The ferryman at Hurst was a thin old man named Ewell Barrow who slept in a shed on the bank and who charged double after midnight, and who did not, in Mira's long experience, ask a single question about anything. He came out with a lantern held high and looked at her and looked at the mare and said nothing at all about the state of either of them.

"River's up," he said. "Rope's taking a lot of strain."

"Can you cross?"

"I can cross. Whether I can cross twice is another matter."

"Then cross once," Mira said, "and go back to bed."

The flat barge rode low and the current pushed at it hard enough that the rope groaned. Mira stood at Tuppence's head with one hand on the mare's nose and murmured whatever nonsense came to her, because the mare hated bridges and had a very reasonable opinion of boats as well. Halfway across, a whole tree came down the river past them, turning slowly, its roots up in the air like a hand. Barrow watched it go and spat over the side.

"Third one tonight," he said. "There'll be more."

On the far bank Mira paid him and asked, as though it did not matter, whether anyone else had crossed that evening. Barrow scratched the side of his jaw and said that two men had come down about ten o'clock wanting the ferry and had not liked his price and had gone off towards Coldbrook instead, and that one of them had a grey horse and a very loud way of talking. Mira thanked him and rode on, and did not say what she was thinking, which was that men who go to Coldbrook to save a penny are not men in a hurry, and men who are not in a hurry are men who already know where you are going.

The road to Ardwen ran between hedges for six miles and then broke out onto open heath. By then the rain had thinned to a fine drizzle and there was a scrap of moon behind the cloud, enough to give the heath a dull silver colour and to turn the standing water into a scatter of small bright coins. Mira let the mare walk. Her back hurt. Her hands were cold through the gloves. She had been in the saddle since four in the afternoon and had eaten nothing since a pie in Bellmarch that had been more pastry than pie.

Ardwen House stood at the end of an avenue of dead elms, and the first thing Mira noticed about it was that there were no lights. The second thing she noticed was that the gate stood open, and had stood open long enough for grass to grow up around the foot of it. She reined in under the trees and looked at the long black face of the building for a while. Twenty windows, and not one of them showing so much as a candle. A wide flight of steps. A door.

She had been given a house that nobody lived in.

Mira tied the mare in the shelter of a wall on the lee side, out of the wind, where she could not be seen from the avenue. She took the satchel and a stub of candle and her knife, and she went up the steps, and the door opened before she touched it, swinging inward on the sort of hinge that has not been oiled since anybody cared.

The hall smelled of damp plaster and old ash. Her candle made a small shivering circle that showed her a staircase, a chequered floor with half its tiles missing, and a long table with a dust sheet over it that somebody had pulled aside. There were footprints in the dust. She crouched and looked at them properly. Two sets, both larger than her own, both coming in from the door and going up the stairs, and one set coming back down again. Nobody had gone out.

She stood and thought about turning around. It was, she told herself afterwards, the sensible thing, and she did think about it, honestly and for several seconds. Then she went up the stairs.

The first floor was a corridor with six doors. Four of them stood open onto rooms full of nothing but shuttered windows and the shapes of furniture under sheets. The fifth was locked. The sixth led to a study, and in the study there was a desk, and on the desk there was a lamp with oil still in it, and beside the lamp there was a chart weighted at its corners with four smooth stones from a beach.

Mira lit the lamp. The chart showed a coastline she half knew, the north shore where the land broke into a scatter of low islands, and somebody had drawn on it in pencil: a line running out from the harbour at Skell, curving north, and ending in a small neat cross with a date beside it. The date was the eleventh of last month. Around the cross, in the same hand, somebody had written the words the Kettle Wren and then, underneath, a question mark, and then, underneath that, pressed so hard the pencil had torn the paper, the word no.

She had heard of the Kettle Wren. Everybody in the county had heard of the Kettle Wren, because a ship that sails out of a small harbour with nineteen men aboard and does not come back is the only thing anyone talks about for a season. The story as Mira had it was that the ship had gone out for the autumn fishing and had been caught by the gale that came in from the north west on the tenth, and that was that. No wreckage. No bodies. Nineteen men, a boat, and a hole in a town.

Under the chart there was a ledger, and in the ledger there were dates and figures and a column of names, and against nine of those names somebody had drawn a small careful tick. Mira turned back three pages and found the same names again, and the same ticks, and a note in the margin about a cargo landed at a place called Rell Point on a night when the Kettle Wren was supposed to have been forty miles out at sea, fishing.

She stood in that cold room with the lamp hissing and worked it through. A ship that was not where it was said to be. A cargo landed quietly on a shingle beach. Nine men out of nineteen who knew, and ten who did not, or who did and would not say. And then a gale, and then a silence, and then a house with no lights and a chart with the word no scratched into it hard enough to tear.

Something moved downstairs.

It was not a large sound. It was the particular hollow knock of a boot on a bare board, and it came from the hall, and it was followed by a second one, and then by the long complaining note of the front door being pushed further open. Mira blew out the lamp. The dark came down over her like a dropped blanket and for a moment she could not have found the door with both hands.

She counted to five and let her eyes adjust and then went out of the study, keeping to the wall where the boards were held down at both ends and were less likely to speak. At the head of the stairs she stopped. There was a light in the hall below, a lantern, and it threw two long shadows up the wall. The voices were low, and they were not trying very hard to be quiet.

"Horse outside," said one. "Tied round the side."

"Then she is here."

"She might be walking about. It is a big house."

"It is a big house," the second man agreed, "and there is one door."

Mira went back along the corridor. The window at the far end was shuttered, and the shutter was warped, and it took her both hands and the flat of her shoulder to get it open without noise, and when it came it came all at once with a crack like a snapped stick. Below her the voices stopped.

The drop was about fourteen feet onto wet grass with a laurel hedge in the way, and she did not think about it for long enough to talk herself out of it. She got her legs over the sill, hung by her hands, and let go. The hedge took most of it and the ground took the rest, and she landed badly on her left ankle and rolled and came up running with her breath sawing and her hands full of leaves.

She got to the mare before they got to the door. She had the reins loose and one foot in the stirrup when the lantern came round the corner of the house, and then she was up and turning and driving Tuppence hard down the avenue between the dead elms with a man shouting behind her and the wet gravel flying.

They came after her. Of course they came after her. She heard them get to their horses and she heard the change in the noise as they reached the road, and she put her head down along the mare's neck and rode.

For two miles the road was straight and she lost nothing and gained nothing. She could hear them back there, steady, not gaining, and she began to understand that they did not need to gain. They were between her and the ferry. They were driving her west, and west along this road there was one bridge, and the bridge was at Coldbrook, and Coldbrook was where the third man would be.

So she left the road.

There was a farm gate on the left with the top bar broken, and she put the mare at it and over it, and then they were in a field of stubble with the mud coming up in great wet gouts and the rain starting again in earnest. The field ran down towards the river. She knew that much. Everything in this country ran down towards the river in the end.

The Lann at that point was forty yards across and running fast and full of the night's rubbish. There was no crossing. There was, however, a towpath, and the towpath ran east along the bank towards Hurst, and if she could get onto it she had a flat surface and a straight run and a mare with more heart than either of those horses behind her.

She got onto it. The bank was steep and the mare went down it half sitting, and for one terrible second Mira thought they were going into the water. Then the hooves found gravel and they were away, and the river was a black roaring wall on her right and the willows came out of the dark one after another and went past.

She rode like that for a mile and a half. The men were behind her on the path now, she could hear them, and they had the advantage of following rather than choosing, and slowly, slowly, they began to come up. She heard a shout. She did not look round. Ahead the path bent left around a stand of alder and there was a bridge over a side channel, a plank thing, and beyond it the path went on.

She was at the bend when the tree came.

It was another whole elm, roots and all, turning down the middle of the river the way the first one had, and it swung in against the outer bank exactly where the bank was undercut, and it took a great bite out of the path perhaps ten yards ahead of her. Mira hauled the mare up so hard that they slid the last of it and the mare's forefeet went over the new edge and scrabbled and came back. In front of her the towpath simply stopped. There was raw earth and roots and then eight feet of nothing and then the path again.

Behind her the two riders came round the alders and pulled up.

For a moment nobody moved. The rain came down. The river tore at the tree and the tree ground against the bank and the whole world was noise.

"There is nowhere to go," the taller of the two called out. He had a reasonable voice, a voice for selling things. "Give us the bag and ride away. Nobody is looking for you."

"You have been looking for me since Bellmarch," Mira said.

"We have been looking for the bag."

"Then you should have paid me to bring it to you."

She was measuring the gap while she talked. Eight feet, perhaps nine, with rotten ground on the near side and nothing to promise the far side was better. Tuppence hated bridges. That was the thought that kept arriving. The mare hated bridges and this was worse than a bridge.

"Whose house is it?" Mira said. "Ardwen. Who owns it?"

There was a pause that told her more than any answer would have.

"It belonged to a man called Hessel," the taller one said at last. "He drowned."

"On the eleventh of last month?"

The second man swore, and that was the whole confirmation, and after that there was no more talking. They came at her along the path at a walk, spreading as far as the path would let them, and Mira turned Tuppence in the small space she had, and backed her three steps, and put her heels in.

The mare went. Whatever she thought about the gap she kept to herself, and she went, and for a long slow moment there was no ground at all, only the sound of the river directly underneath, and then the far bank came up and hit them and the mare's hind legs went in to the hock and she heaved, and heaved again, and came out of it, and they were through and running with mud to the girth and Mira's whole body shaking.

She did not stop until Hurst. Barrow came out of his shed with the lantern and looked at the pair of them, the mare with her head down and her sides going like a bellows, the woman covered to the chin in river mud, and he said nothing about that either.

"Cross," Mira said. "Now. And then cut the rope."

"That rope cost me nine shillings."

"There are two men behind me who will pay you a great deal more than nine shillings to bring them over, and then they will kill you, because you will have seen them."

Barrow thought about that for exactly as long as it deserved, and then he untied the barge.

They crossed with the current shoving and the rope shrieking, and on the western bank Barrow took his axe from under the seat and cut the rope through in four strokes and stood and watched the free end whip out and down and vanish into the black water. Then he sat down on the bank and put the axe across his knees and looked at the river as if it had done him a personal injury.

"Nine shillings," he said.

Mira gave him a great deal more than nine shillings. Then she rode north.

She reached Skell at dawn with the sky going the colour of wet slate over the sea and the gulls already up and arguing. The harbour was small and grey and full of boats that had not gone out. She found the harbourmaster's office above the chandlery, and she waited on the stairs for an hour until a heavy woman with grey plaits came up and unlocked the door, and then Mira put the satchel on the desk in front of her and broke the wax with her thumb, which was the first rule of the trade and the only one she had never broken until that morning.

Inside there was a bundle of letters tied with string, and a list of nine names, and a signed statement in a careful clerk's hand describing a cargo landed at Rell Point on a night in October when the Kettle Wren was supposed to have been at sea. And underneath all of it there was a short note, unsigned, that said only that the writer had been aboard, that the ship had not sunk in the gale, and that he was the only one of the nineteen who had got ashore.

The harbourmaster read it twice. She was not a woman who wept, and she did not weep then, but she put both hands flat on the desk and stayed like that for a while with her head down.

"Ten men had families here," she said. "Ten. And nine of them had a share in whatever this was."

"The tenth," Mira said. "The one who got ashore. Where would he go?"

"Ardwen," said the harbourmaster. "Hessel's house. He worked for Hessel." She looked up. "But Hessel drowned."

"Hessel," said Mira, "may have drowned. But somebody has been living in that house with the shutters closed, and burning lamp oil, and drawing on a chart, and the last thing he wrote on it was the word no."

She went out into the grey morning and stood on the harbour wall and looked at the water, which was flat now and innocent and the colour of an old coin, as though it had never done anything to anyone. Behind her the town was beginning to wake up. Somewhere a door banged and a dog started and a woman called out to somebody about the bread.

Mira went down to the stable where she had left the mare and found her asleep on her feet with her nose in an empty manger, filthy to the shoulder and entirely unbothered. She fetched an apple from the yard and held it out flat on her palm, and the mare woke up and took it with enormous seriousness and dropped half of it on the ground.

There was a road back to Ardwen and she was going to take it, and she was going to take the harbourmaster and four large men from the chandlery with her, and she was going to go through that house room by room until she found the locked door on the first floor and whoever was behind it. But that was for the afternoon. For an hour she stood in the warm dark of the stable with her hand on the mare's neck, listening to the rain come on again outside, and thought about nothing much at all.
