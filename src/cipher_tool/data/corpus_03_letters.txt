Sir, I have the honour to report that the column under my command reached the crossing at Fen Bridge shortly after first light on the morning of the eleventh, and that the passage of the river was effected without loss of men, horses or stores. The water stood higher than the survey of last autumn had led us to expect, and the fording place marked upon the older maps proved wholly impassable for wheeled transport. I therefore directed the engineer detachment to lay a temporary roadway of timber and brushwood some four hundred yards downstream, where the banks are firm and the current slackens against a shoal of gravel. The work occupied the greater part of the day. By evening the guns, the ammunition carts and the whole of the baggage had crossed, and the men were under canvas upon the northern shore. I beg leave to commend the conduct of the engineers, whose officers stood in the water for six hours together without relief and without a word of complaint.

With reference to your message of the fourteenth, I regret that I am not yet in a position to give the assurance you require regarding the onward march. The state of the roads beyond the bridge is very much worse than the district reports had suggested. Three days of rain have turned the low ground into a soft black mire in which a loaded waggon sinks to the axle within a hundred paces. I have set two companies to the work of cutting and laying timber, and I estimate that a passable track of some two miles can be made good by the evening of the seventeenth. Should the weather hold, I propose to move at dawn on the eighteenth and to reach the market town of Aldermere by the following night. If the rain returns, I must ask for leave to halt where I am until the ground recovers. I would rather arrive late with an intact column than early with half my transport abandoned in a field.

I have further to report upon the state of supply, which gives me some concern. The ration issue at present stands at nine days for bread and eleven for salt meat, but the forage is very short, and I have been obliged to reduce the allowance for the draught animals by a quarter. There is no hay to be bought in the district at any price. The farmers tell me, and I believe them, that the harvest was poor and that what remains has already been taken up by the commissary of the neighbouring district. I have accordingly sent Captain Ridley with a small party and a written authority to purchase in the villages along the eastern road, with strict instructions that everything taken is to be paid for in coin at the local rate and entered in the receipt book. I attach a copy of his instructions for your information. I would be obliged if you would confirm that the sums so expended may be charged against the general account of the command.

Of the enemy I can say little that is certain. Patrols sent out on the twelfth and thirteenth found the country empty as far as the ridge above the mill. A herdsman, questioned through an interpreter, spoke of a body of horse passing eastward some six days ago, but he could give no useful estimate of their number, and I do not place great weight upon the account. The bridge at Coldwater has been burnt, and burnt recently, for the timbers were still warm when my scouts reached them. I take this to mean that the withdrawal is deliberate and orderly rather than hurried. I shall report again as soon as I have anything more solid than the talk of the countryside. Please acknowledge receipt of this despatch and of the two returns enclosed with it.

Your letter of the ninth instant reached this office on the morning of the fifteenth, and I am directed to acknowledge it and to thank you for the fullness of the information it contains. The Commander in Chief has read your report with attention and desires me to convey his approval of the measures you took at the crossing. He further directs me to say that the discretion for which you ask is granted, and that you may halt the column at your own judgement should the weather make movement wasteful of men and animals. He asks only that you keep this headquarters informed by the daily courier, and that you give the earliest possible warning of any intention to alter your line of march. Two hundred sacks of oats have been ordered forward from the depot at Wraysbury and should reach you by the twentieth. The bread contract for the district has been placed with a new supplier, and I am to warn you that the first deliveries may be irregular in quality.

The following memorandum is issued for the guidance of all officers commanding detachments within the northern district. Winter clothing will be distributed from the twenty second of the present month, and commanding officers are requested to render a return of requirements not later than the twentieth. The return should show, for each detachment, the number of greatcoats, blankets, pairs of boots and woollen shirts actually required, and not the establishment figure. Experience of last year showed that returns made upon the establishment produced a serious waste of stores at some posts and a real shortage at others. Officers are reminded that boots are to be issued by measurement and not by guess, and that a man in boots that do not fit him is very soon a man in hospital. Any deficiency discovered after the general issue will be made good only upon a written application signed by the officer commanding, stating the cause of the deficiency.

Telegram received at two forty in the afternoon and here written out in full for the record. The relief party has arrived safely at the station and reports all well. Two waggons were lost on the descent from the pass and one driver was injured, though not seriously, and he is now in the care of the surgeon. The road above the second bend is broken for a length of about sixty yards and cannot be used by wheeled traffic until repaired. Please send forward picks, shovels, a quantity of iron cramps and, if it can be spared, a competent mason. I propose to remain here until the road is made good. Reply by the same means and confirm whether the second convoy is to be held at the depot or sent forward by the lower route, which is longer by eleven miles but sound throughout.

The Legation presents its compliments to the Ministry and has the honour to refer to the Ministry's note of the third of this month concerning the passage of merchant vessels through the northern channel. The Legation is instructed to state that its Government has considered the proposals with the closest attention and in a spirit of goodwill. It welcomes in particular the suggestion that a joint commission be established to survey the channel and to agree upon a common system of marks and lights. Upon the question of dues, however, the Legation is obliged to observe that the scale proposed appears to bear more heavily upon vessels of shallow draught than upon larger ships, and that the greater part of the traffic of its own merchants is carried in vessels of the smaller class. The Legation would therefore be glad if the Ministry would consider a revision of the scale before the matter is laid before the commission. The Legation avails itself of this opportunity to renew the assurance of its high consideration.

The Ministry has the honour to acknowledge the note of the Legation of the seventeenth and to state that the observations contained in it have been laid before the competent department. The Ministry is happy to inform the Legation that the department sees no objection in principle to a revision of the scale of dues, and that a draft embodying such a revision will be communicated within a fortnight. The Ministry would take this occasion to raise a separate matter of some urgency. Three fishing boats belonging to citizens of this country were detained last week in the eastern roadstead and have not yet been released. The Ministry does not at this stage question the grounds of the detention, but it would be grateful for an early statement of the facts, and it trusts that the crews at least may be permitted to return to their families while the legal question is examined.

I have the honour to submit the annual report upon the trade of this port for the year now ended. The total tonnage entering the harbour was rather more than one hundred and forty thousand tons, an increase of about one part in twelve upon the preceding year. The greater part of the increase is accounted for by the coasting trade in coal and timber, the deep sea traffic having remained very nearly stationary. Exports of wool were disappointing. The clip was good and the quality high, but buyers held back through the spring in the expectation of lower prices, and a considerable quantity was still in the warehouses at the close of the year. Grain shipments were heavier than usual, and the new elevator at the eastern quay has proved its worth, loading a vessel of two thousand tons in a little under two days. I would draw attention once more to the state of the outer breakwater, which was damaged in the gales of the winter before last and has not been properly repaired. A single heavy sea from the north east would, in my opinion, carry away a further length of the parapet and endanger shipping lying inside.

Dear Sirs, we have to acknowledge the arrival of the consignment shipped by you on the twenty eighth, and we regret that we cannot report it in satisfactory condition. Of the forty cases landed, seven had been broken open and roughly repaired before delivery, and the contents of three of these are so badly stained by sea water as to be unsaleable. We have had the whole consignment examined by a sworn surveyor, whose certificate we enclose, and we have entered a formal protest with the agents of the vessel. We do not for a moment suggest that the fault lies with your packing, which has always been careful, and the surveyor is of the opinion that the damage occurred in the ship's hold and not in the warehouse. We shall of course pursue the claim against the carriers, but as the goods were sold to us on terms which place the risk upon the seller until delivery, we must ask you in the first instance to make good the loss. A statement of the amount is enclosed.

Dear Sirs, we have received your letter reporting damage to the consignment and we are sorry indeed to learn of it. We accept without reserve the finding of the surveyor and we do not propose to dispute your claim. A credit note for the full amount stated has been passed to your account today, and you will find it entered upon the next statement. We should be grateful if you would send us the broken cases, carriage forward, since our packers wish to examine the method of construction and to satisfy themselves that the fastenings are strong enough for a winter passage. We take this opportunity to say that we have insured the next shipment against all risks and that the premium has been borne by ourselves. Business of many years' standing is not worth losing for the sake of a few pounds, and we hope that this may be the end of the matter.

To the underwriters, gentlemen, I am instructed by my clients to give formal notice of a claim under the policy issued by you on the twelfth of March last, covering the vessel and cargo upon a voyage from this port to the northern coast and back. The vessel was driven ashore in the gale of the second and lies at present upon a sandbank about a mile below the harbour mouth. She is not, in the opinion of the master, beyond saving, and salvage operations have already begun under the direction of a firm of contractors whose estimate is enclosed. Of the cargo, some three fifths has been landed in a damaged state and the remainder is still in the hold. A full statement of particulars will be furnished as soon as the survey is complete. In the meantime my clients wish to place on record that every reasonable step is being taken to reduce the loss, and they trust that the expenditure now being incurred will be allowed in due course.

The board met on the afternoon of the twenty fifth, the chairman presiding, and I am directed to circulate the following memorandum to shareholders. The accounts for the half year show a modest profit after the usual provision for depreciation, and the directors recommend a dividend at the rate agreed at the last general meeting. The improvement is due chiefly to the reduction of working costs at the northern works, where the new machinery installed in the spring has more than repaid its cost. Against this must be set a decline in the value of stock held at the southern depot, arising from the fall in prices in the early summer. The directors have thought it prudent to write this stock down to the present market value rather than to carry it forward at cost. They are unable at this stage to offer any forecast for the second half of the year, the state of trade being uncertain and the outcome of the wage negotiations not yet known.

Dear Sir, it is with real regret that I ask you to accept my resignation from the position I have held in this department for the past nine years. My reasons are entirely private and have nothing to do with the work or with the treatment I have received, which has throughout been generous. My wife's health requires that we move to a milder part of the country before the winter, and I have been fortunate enough to find employment near the place we have chosen. I should like to leave at the end of the quarter, which gives rather more than the notice required, and I shall of course do everything I can in the meantime to hand over my duties in good order. The files relating to the harbour contract are the only ones in which I hold information not fully recorded, and I propose to spend part of next week setting that record straight. I am grateful for many kindnesses and I shall watch the fortunes of the department with a warm interest.

The board of inquiry appointed to examine the circumstances of the fire at the eastern warehouse has completed its sittings and submits the following findings. The fire was first observed at about a quarter past eleven at night by the watchman on his second round, and the alarm was given without delay. The brigade arrived within eleven minutes and had water upon the building within fifteen. Despite this the roof of the older wing fell in shortly after midnight and the whole of the stock stored beneath it was destroyed. The board finds that the fire began in the loft above the packing room and that its most probable cause was the overheating of a flue which passes through that loft and which had not been swept for upwards of two years. The board finds no evidence whatever of negligence on the part of the watchman, whose conduct was prompt and sensible, and it wishes to record that he entered the burning building at some risk to himself in order to release two horses stabled at the northern end.

The board makes the following recommendations. All flues in buildings used for storage should be swept twice in each year and a record of the sweeping kept in the manager's office. The internal doors between the packing room and the main store should be replaced with doors of iron, and the openings reduced in width. A second hydrant should be laid on at the eastern corner of the yard, where at present the hose must be run out for a distance which wastes both time and pressure. Finally, the board is of the opinion that the practice of storing oil and cotton waste in the same building as finished goods is indefensible, and it recommends that a small detached store be built for that purpose in the far corner of the yard. The board wishes to thank the officers of the brigade for their assistance and for the clear evidence they gave.

I have the honour to forward the weekly return of sick and wounded for the field hospital under my charge. The total number of patients now under treatment is one hundred and thirty one, of whom eighteen are wounded and the remainder sick. The prevailing complaints are fever and disorders of the stomach, and I have no hesitation in attributing both to the water drawn from the shallow wells near the lower camp. I have twice reported upon this water and I must report upon it again. Boiling is ordered, but boiling is not carried out with any regularity when the men are tired and the fuel is short. I would urge most strongly that the pipe from the upper spring be repaired without further delay. The cost would be small and the saving in sickness very great. Of the wounded, all are doing well except two, whose condition remains grave, and I have written separately to their families as the regulations require.

I have the honour to report upon the progress of the works at the new landing stage. The piling is complete for two thirds of the length, and the timber decking has been laid over the first eighty feet. Work was interrupted for four days by high water and for two more by the failure of a crane, which has since been repaired. Notwithstanding these delays I am confident that the stage will be ready for use by the end of the second week in November, that is to say a little in advance of the date named in the contract. The stone for the approach road has arrived and is stacked upon the foreshore. I have to report one difficulty. The depth alongside the outer end of the stage is less by two feet than the plans show, and unless some dredging is done the larger vessels will be unable to lie there at low water. I have taken soundings at three states of the tide and enclose the figures. An early decision upon the question of dredging would be of great assistance.

I have the honour to report the state of the negotiations at the close of the present week. The two delegations have now met on five occasions and have reached agreement upon the greater part of the commercial articles. The question of tariffs upon manufactured goods, which was expected to give the most trouble, was settled with surprising ease on Wednesday, both sides accepting the formula proposed by the smaller delegation. Two matters remain in dispute. The first is the treatment of goods carried in the vessels of third countries, upon which the two positions are still far apart. The second, and in my judgement the more serious, is the duration of the agreement. Our own side has proposed a term of ten years with provision for review; the other side presses for five years and for a right of denunciation upon six months' notice. I shall telegraph as soon as there is anything decided. In the meantime I would be glad of instructions upon the point of duration, since I am not willing to concede it without authority.

The following is issued for general information and will be read to all ranks at the first parade. The command wishes to place on record its appreciation of the conduct of the troops during the difficult movement of the past three weeks. The marching has been hard, the weather has been against us throughout, and the supply of food and forage has more than once fallen below what was intended. In these conditions the discipline of the force has been excellent and the behaviour of the men towards the inhabitants of the country has been correct in every particular. Not a single complaint of any substance has been received. This is a matter of more importance than may at first appear, for the goodwill of the district is worth a great deal to us and would be very hard to recover once lost. Officers are asked to make this known to their men in plain terms and to say that it has been noticed.
