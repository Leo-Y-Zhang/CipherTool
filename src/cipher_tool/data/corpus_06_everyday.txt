The rain started before six this morning and it has not really stopped since. I lay in bed for a while listening to it hitting the flat roof of the kitchen extension, which makes a sound like someone slowly emptying a bag of dried peas onto a tin tray. There is a gap in the gutter above the back door and the water comes down in a rope rather than a trickle, so the step outside is always the first thing to flood. I got up at half past six, put the kettle on and stood in the doorway with a mug of tea while the sky went from black to a sort of dirty grey. The neighbours' cat was sitting under the hedge looking miserable and refusing to come in out of the wet, which is the same thing she does every single week. I gave her a bit of ham and she took it and stayed exactly where she was.

The bus was late again, which nobody was surprised about. There were nine of us at the stop by the time it came, all standing slightly apart under the shelter and pretending not to notice each other, and the man from the flat above the chemist kept checking the timetable on the pole as if reading it harder might make the bus arrive. When it finally pulled in the driver apologised through the little window and said there had been roadworks on the bridge, and a woman near the front said it was fine, love, we all know what the bridge is like. I got a seat at the back and watched the town go past through a window that was so misted up I had to keep wiping it with my sleeve. The fields on the far side of the retail park had water standing in them in long silver strips.

Work at the moment is busy but not unpleasant. I am on the second floor of a building that used to be a printing works, and you can still see where the big machines were bolted down because the concrete has pale rectangles in it under the carpet tiles. There are twenty two of us in the office and we sit at long shared desks with low screens between them, which is meant to encourage people to talk to each other and mostly encourages people to put on headphones. My job is to check the orders that come in from the shops, chase the ones that look wrong, and let the warehouse know when something needs to be sent out early. It sounds dull written down like that, and some days it is, but there is a real satisfaction in clearing a list of problems one at a time until the screen is empty and you can go home.

The building has a kitchen at the end of the corridor with two microwaves, a fridge that hums, and a laminated notice above the sink asking people to wash up their own things. Nobody obeys the notice. Every Friday afternoon somebody, usually Nadia from the accounts team, gets fed up and does a big wash up while complaining loudly enough for the whole floor to hear, and for about three days afterwards the sink stays clean. Then it starts again. We have a rota for making the tea in the morning which works better, partly because everyone actually wants the tea. There is a biscuit tin that is refilled by whoever is last to notice that it is empty, and an unwritten rule that you never take the last one in a packet without saying so out loud.

The primary school on the other side of the park held its open evening on Tuesday and I went along with my sister to have a look, because her eldest starts in September. The classrooms smell exactly the way they did when I was small, a mixture of poster paint, floor polish and damp coats. There were paintings of boats pegged to a line across one wall and a table of things the children had made out of egg boxes and silver foil, each with a small card giving the name of the maker in careful, wobbly capitals. A teacher called Miss Hollows showed us round the reception class and explained how the day is split up, with reading first thing, then number work, then a long stretch in the afternoon where the children choose what they do from a set of tables. My niece went straight for the sand tray and had to be prised away from it at the end.

In the hall there was a display about a project the older children had done on the river that runs behind the school. They had drawn a map of it from the bridge down to the weir, marked on the places where herons had been seen, and written short pieces about what they had found in the water when they went out with nets. One boy had written that he had caught a stickleback and put it back because it looked worried, which I thought was the best line of writing in the entire building. The head teacher gave a short talk in which she thanked the parents who had helped with the trips, said that the roof over the year two classroom was finally being repaired in the summer holidays, and asked, in the politest possible way, for people to stop parking across the gate at half past three.

The new footbridge over the railway opened on Saturday morning, four months later than planned and roughly a third over the original budget. About sixty people came out for the opening, most of them standing under umbrellas at the north end while the deputy mayor cut a ribbon that had gone limp in the rain. The bridge replaces the old level crossing, which had been closed to walkers for nearly two years after a survey found that the barriers were failing to lower properly on about one train in forty. Residents on the estate behind the station have been asking for a replacement crossing since the closure, because the alternative route to the shops adds nearly a mile in each direction and takes an elderly walker the better part of half an hour.

Speaking after the opening, a member of the parish council said that the delay had been caused mainly by the discovery of an old drainage culvert under the embankment, which had to be surveyed and partly rebuilt before the foundations could go in. She added that while nobody was pleased about the extra cost, the alternative would have been a structure that started to sink within a decade, and that the council had taken the view that it was better to do the work once and do it properly. A local shopkeeper said that trade on the estate side had fallen noticeably during the closure and that he expected it to pick up now that people could walk over in three minutes. The bridge has a ramp at each end rather than steps, which several people mentioned as the detail that mattered most to them.

I went to the cafe on the corner of the market square on Thursday, mostly because it was raining and I had twenty minutes to fill before the dentist. It is a narrow room with six tables, a counter at the back, and shelves along one wall holding jars of things that are for sale and things that are clearly just decoration. The coffee was good, strong without being bitter, and came in a heavy cup that kept it warm for longer than usual. I had a slice of lemon cake that was slightly too sweet but had a proper crumb to it, not the damp claggy texture you get from a factory. The woman behind the counter was doing four things at once and still managed to ask everyone how they were, and to mean it. There is a shelf of secondhand paperbacks by the door with an honesty box, which I liked.

The only thing I would change is the seating, because the chairs are those thin metal ones that look lovely and become uncomfortable after about ten minutes. That may well be deliberate on a busy market day. Prices are fair for the town, a pound or so more than the big chain by the station and worth it. They do a soup and a roll at lunchtime that changes every day and is written up on a board in chalk, and when I went past on Friday it was carrot and coriander, which had already sold out by one o'clock. If you want a table by the window at the weekend you need to be there before ten. Otherwise you will end up at the little round one by the kitchen door, which is fine unless you mind being brushed past every two minutes.

On Saturday evening we went to see a play at the arts centre, a two hander called The Long Way Round that was written by somebody local. The set was almost nothing, just two chairs, a coat stand and a screen at the back that changed colour to mark the passing of time, and it worked far better than a complicated set would have done. The story follows a brother and sister clearing out their late father's house over a single weekend, and most of the humour comes from the arguments they have about which objects are worth keeping. There is a very good scene involving a broken clock. The two actors were excellent, particularly the woman playing the sister, who managed to be irritating and sympathetic at the same time without ever pushing it too far. It ran for ninety minutes with no interval, which was exactly right.

I would say it is worth an evening if it comes anywhere near you, though the ending is a little too tidy for my taste. The audience was about two thirds full, mostly people over fifty, and the bar afterwards was doing a steady trade in bottled beer and small glasses of wine. Parking at the arts centre is free after six, which the website does not make very clear, and there is a car park behind the library that is closer than the one they signpost. Tickets were twelve pounds each with a reduction for students, and there was a note in the programme thanking the volunteers who run the box office. I put a couple of coins in the bucket on the way out because the whole building runs on that sort of thing.

If you have never mended a puncture on a bicycle, it is a good deal easier than it looks and the whole job takes about a quarter of an hour once you have done it two or three times. Start by turning the bike upside down so it rests on the saddle and handlebars, and put something soft underneath if you care about the paint. Let out any air that is left in the tube by pressing the little pin in the valve. Then work the tyre lever under the edge of the tyre, hook it onto a spoke, and use a second lever a few inches along to lift the rest of the tyre off the rim. You only need to get one side off. Pull the tube out gently, leaving the valve until last, and push the valve down through the hole in the rim rather than dragging it sideways.

Now pump a little air into the tube and listen for the hiss, or pass it slowly close to your cheek, which is more reliable than you would expect. If you cannot find the hole that way, dip the tube in a bowl of water and watch for bubbles. Mark the spot with the chalk from the repair kit. Roughen the rubber around the hole with the little square of sandpaper, spread a thin layer of glue over an area rather larger than the patch, and then wait. Waiting is the part everyone gets wrong. The glue needs three or four minutes until it is tacky rather than wet, and if you press the patch on too early it will lift off on the first ride. Press it down hard for a minute, peel off the backing, and leave it for a few minutes more before you put everything back.

Before you refit the tube, run your fingers all the way round the inside of the tyre. Do it slowly, and do it carefully, because there is very often a thorn or a shard of glass still sitting in the rubber, and if you leave it there you will be doing the whole job again by the end of the week. Put a small amount of air into the tube so that it holds its shape, feed the valve through the rim first, and tuck the rest of the tube inside the tyre all the way round. Then work the tyre back over the rim with your thumbs, starting opposite the valve and finishing at it. Try to avoid the levers at this stage, since they are the most common way of pinching the tube and creating a second puncture. Pump it up, check the tyre is seated evenly, and you are done.

Soup is the thing I cook most often in the winter, and leek and potato is the one I come back to because it uses whatever is in the bottom of the vegetable drawer. Take three good sized leeks, cut off the roots and the toughest of the dark green, and slice the rest into rounds about the thickness of a pound coin. Wash them properly in a bowl of cold water, because leeks hold grit between their layers and nothing ruins a smooth soup like sand. Melt a generous knob of butter in a heavy pan over a low heat, add the leeks with a pinch of salt, and let them soften for ten minutes without colouring. Stir them now and then. While that is happening, peel and dice about five hundred grams of floury potatoes into pieces roughly the size of a walnut.

Add the potatoes to the pan, stir them through the buttery leeks for a minute or two, and then pour in enough stock to cover everything by a finger's width. Bring it up to a simmer, put the lid on at an angle, and leave it alone for twenty minutes or until the potato falls apart when you press it against the side of the pan with a spoon. Blend it if you like it smooth, or mash it roughly with a fork if you prefer some texture, which is what I usually do. Taste it before you add anything else, because stock varies enormously in how salty it is. A splash of milk or cream at the end will soften the flavour, and a good grind of black pepper lifts it. It keeps for three days in the fridge and freezes well in old takeaway boxes.

Bread rolls are worth learning for the same reason, which is that they cost almost nothing and make a house smell wonderful. Mix five hundred grams of strong white flour with a teaspoon and a half of salt and a sachet of fast action yeast, keeping the salt and the yeast on opposite sides of the bowl until you add the water. Pour in about three hundred millilitres of warm water and a tablespoon of oil, and bring it together with your hand until there is no dry flour left. Tip it onto a clean surface and knead for ten minutes, pushing the dough away from you with the heel of your hand and folding it back over. It will be sticky at first and then suddenly smooth. Leave it in a covered bowl until it has doubled, which takes an hour in a warm kitchen and rather longer in mine.

Last month we took the train down to the coast for a few days, which is a journey of about two and a half hours with one change at a junction station where the only thing to do is look at the flower tubs on the platform. The line runs inland for the first hour through farmland and small brick villages, and then swings round a headland and drops down beside the water so suddenly that everyone in the carriage looks up. There is a stretch of maybe four miles where the track sits on a sea wall with the beach directly below it, and on a rough day the spray comes right over the windows. Children stand up on the seats for that bit. We had booked a room above a pub in the old part of the town, up a lane so narrow that the delivery vans have to reverse the whole length of it.

The town itself is built on a hill in the way that harbour towns often are, with the newer streets at the top and the oldest ones tumbling down towards the quay in a tangle of steps and passages. We spent the first afternoon simply walking, which is the best way to work out how a place fits together. There is a fish market at the bottom that starts far too early in the morning and is packed away by nine, a church with a squat tower and a wonderful view from the graveyard, and at least four shops selling almost identical buckets and spades. The harbour wall runs out in a long curve and you can walk to the end of it, past men fishing with rods propped in holders and a sign warning you not to do exactly that in a heavy swell.

On the second day we walked the cliff path north as far as the old lighthouse, which is about six miles there and back and takes longer than the distance suggests because of the climbing. The path is well kept, mostly grass with occasional wooden steps set into the steepest sections, and there are benches at the top of each rise with small brass plates on them. We stopped at every single bench. The sea below was that flat, hard blue you only get on a bright day in early spring, and there were gulls hanging almost still in the wind coming up the face of the cliff. Halfway along we passed a farm where the fields run right to the edge, and a horse came over to the fence to see whether we had anything worth eating. We had a bag of apples and shared one with it.

The lighthouse itself is no longer manned and the cottages beside it have been sold off and painted a very determined shade of white. There is a small cafe in what used to be the store room, open at weekends, selling tea, flapjack and postcards. We sat outside with our backs against the warm wall and ate our sandwiches while a couple with two spaniels asked us whether the path continued round the point, which it does, though there is a section where it has slipped and you have to go inland along the road for a quarter of a mile. Coming back we took the lower track, which runs behind the dunes and is sheltered enough that you can hear larks. My legs ached that evening in the pleasant way that means you have done something with your day.

Back at work on the Monday I had a hundred and forty messages waiting, which is what a few days away always costs. The warehouse had run into a problem with a supplier whose lorries kept arriving outside the booked slots, and there had been a certain amount of shouting about it in my absence. We had a meeting about it on the Tuesday in the small room at the end of the corridor, six of us round a table meant for four, with the blinds down because the sun comes straight in at that hour. The outcome was that we would give the supplier one written warning, move their slot to the quieter part of the afternoon, and review it in a month. Meetings like that are much better when somebody writes down who is doing what before everyone leaves the room, and I have started volunteering to be that person.

The warehouse is a fifteen minute drive out on the industrial estate, and I go over every couple of weeks because problems that seem impossible on a screen turn out to be obvious when you stand next to the shelf. It is a huge shed with a roof so high that the lights hang down on long chains, and the noise is a constant mixture of tape guns, trolley wheels and the radio somebody keeps just loud enough to be legal. The team there work in three shifts and know the stock far better than any of us in the office do. Dennis, who has been there since it opened, can tell you which aisle a thing is in without looking it up, and can also tell you which of our product codes are wrong, of which there are more than I would like to admit.

Saturday mornings at the allotment have become the fixed point of my week. I took the plot on two winters ago when it was chest high in brambles and I very nearly gave it back within a month. The first year I got potatoes, courgettes and an enormous number of runner beans, and lost absolutely everything else to slugs, pigeons or my own forgetfulness. This year I have been more careful. There are onions in the bed nearest the path, broad beans behind them, and two rows of carrots under a low tunnel of fine mesh, which is the only thing that keeps the carrot fly off. The shed leans slightly to the left and smells of creosote and old newspaper. There is a kettle in it and a jar of teabags, and on a cold morning that shed is one of the best places I know.

The other plot holders are a mixed lot and almost uniformly generous. If you are new they will give you seed, spare canes, and a great deal of advice, some of which contradicts the rest. The man on the plot below mine grows nothing but dahlias and sweet peas and wins prizes for them at the show in August. The couple at the far end have a greenhouse heated by an old paraffin lamp and produce tomatoes a full month before anybody else. Every autumn there is a swap where people bring what they have too much of and take away what they lack, and every autumn somebody arrives with a wheelbarrow of marrows that nobody wants. I have learned to accept only what I will actually eat, which took me two seasons to work out.

I rang my grandmother on Sunday afternoon as usual. She is eighty seven and lives on her own in the same house she moved into as a young woman, and she is very firm about not being fussed over. We talked about the weather, the state of the front hedge, and a programme she had watched about steam engines that she thought I would like. She told me, as she does every few weeks, the story about the time the river came up over the road and her father carried the piano upstairs on his own, which cannot possibly be true and which I would not correct for anything in the world. She asked after my job, remembered the names of two people I mentioned six months ago, and told me I sounded tired. Before she rang off she said the daffodils along her path had come up thicker than last year.

The heavy rain in the middle of the month brought the river up to within a foot of the top of the flood wall, and for two days the town was full of people wandering down to look at it. The council opened the sandbag store on the Wednesday evening and about thirty volunteers filled and stacked bags outside the properties on the low side of the market. In the end the water came over the wall in only one place, behind the car park, and drained away by the following afternoon. Two shops had water through the floor and one had to close for a fortnight while the boards dried out. A spokesman for the drainage board said the new upstream storage scheme had held back a considerable volume and that without it the outcome would have been worse, while several residents pointed out that they had been promised the same scheme for eleven years before it was built.

I finally replaced my walking boots after the old pair let water in on both feet in the same afternoon, which is a fairly clear message. The new ones are a mid weight leather pair from the shop on the high street where they still measure your foot properly and make you walk up and down a little ramp. They cost more than I wanted to spend and I expect them to last six or seven years, which works out cheaper than buying a soft pair every other winter. They needed about three weeks of wearing round the town before they stopped rubbing at the heel, and I would say to anyone buying boots that this is normal and not a fault. Waterproofing them once a season with wax takes ten minutes and makes an enormous difference. The laces they came with are already fraying, which seems to be true of every boot ever sold.

Putting up a tent in the dark is a skill best learned in daylight, and the way to avoid trouble is to practise once in the garden before you go anywhere. Lay the tent out flat with the door facing away from the wind, and peg the four corners of the groundsheet down before you do anything else. Thread the poles through the sleeves rather than pushing them, because pushing splits the joints and eventually you will lose one in the middle of a field. Raise the poles gradually and clip the inner tent to them, then throw the flysheet over and match the coloured tabs to the right corners. Peg the guy lines out at about forty five degrees, not straight down, and give them a firm tug so the fabric is taut but not stretched. Finally, walk round the whole thing once and check that no part of the flysheet is touching the inner.

Exam season at the secondary school where my cousin teaches begins in a fortnight, and the whole building has changed its rhythm already. The hall has been cleared and filled with rows of single desks, each with a card taped to the corner, and the corridor outside has notices asking for silence between certain hours. She says the hardest part of her job at this time of year is not the teaching but the reassuring, because a great many capable students convince themselves in the last month that they know nothing at all. Her advice to them is always the same and always unwelcome, which is to spend less time making beautiful revision timetables and more time doing questions under a clock. Past papers, marked honestly, tell you exactly where the gaps are. Everything else, she says, is just moving the problem around the desk.

The library reopened in February after its refurbishment and is a great deal better than it was. They have moved the children's section away from the front door so that the noise no longer travels the whole length of the room, put in a row of study desks with proper lamps and sockets, and replaced the carpet that had been there since long before I first had a ticket. The reading group meets on the first Tuesday of the month in the small room at the back, and I have been going since the autumn. There are usually nine or ten of us and the discussion is at least half about things other than the book. Last month we read a novel about a lighthouse keeper that three people loved and four people found unbearably slow, and the argument about it was much more enjoyable than the book itself.

This evening I have done very little and I do not feel especially guilty about it. I made the soup, ate it with two of the rolls from yesterday, and washed up while the radio talked about something I only half listened to. The dog has been asleep by the radiator since seven and has twitched his back legs twice, chasing something in whatever dogs dream about. The rain that started this morning has settled into that fine steady sort that you barely notice until you step outside and find you are wet through. Tomorrow I need to take the car in for its service, ring the plumber about the noise the boiler makes when the heating comes on, and get the parcel to the post office before it shuts at half past five. None of it is difficult. All of it will take longer than I think.

Looking back over the last few months, what strikes me is how much of a life is made up of small repeated things and how little of it is made up of the events you would put in a summary. The bus, the kettle, the walk across the park, the list on the fridge with three things crossed off and two added. I used to find that idea a bit depressing and I do not any more. The trips to the coast and the evenings at the theatre are good, and I want more of them, but they only mean anything because of the ordinary weeks either side. Next month the days will be properly long again and I can get to the allotment before work rather than after. That is the plan, anyway, and I have written it down here so that I will feel obliged to try.
