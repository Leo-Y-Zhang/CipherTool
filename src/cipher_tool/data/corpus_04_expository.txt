Almost every machine that people depend upon can be understood, at least in outline, by asking a single stubborn question: where does the energy come from, and where does it eventually go? The question sounds simple, and in a sense it is, but it has the useful property of refusing to be answered vaguely, because energy cannot be created by enthusiasm or destroyed by neglect, and so any explanation that leaves a gap in the accounting is an explanation that has quietly gone wrong somewhere. When you trace the path of energy through a device, from the fuel or the battery or the falling water at one end, through the shafts and gears and wires in the middle, to the useful work and the inevitable waste heat at the other, the shape of the machine stops being arbitrary. Every part begins to look like an answer to a problem. The thick casing is there because something inside is trying to fly apart; the fins on the outside are there because something inside is getting hot and must be persuaded to give that heat away; the odd bulge on the side houses a pump that exists only because a fluid somewhere refuses to circulate on its own. Nothing in a well made mechanism is decorative. It may be ugly, and it is often more complicated than it needs to be, but it is rarely pointless, and the habit of assuming that each component earns its place is the beginning of understanding.

Consider the lever, which is the oldest machine of all and the one from which most of the others descend. A lever does not create force out of nothing, and anyone who thinks it does has misunderstood the bargain, because what the lever actually does is trade distance for force in a strictly fair exchange. If you push the long end down through a metre and the short end lifts a heavy stone through a tenth of that, then the force at the stone is roughly ten times the force at your hands, and the product of force and distance, which is the work done, comes out very nearly the same on both sides. The small shortfall is friction, and friction is simply the portion of your effort that has been converted into warmth in the pivot rather than height in the stone. Gears are levers that have been curled into circles and set spinning, so that the trade can be made continuously instead of once. A small gear driving a large one turns quickly and weakly, and the large one turns slowly and strongly, which is why the low gear on a bicycle lets a tired rider climb a hill that would otherwise defeat them, at the cost of pedalling furiously to travel very little distance. Pulleys, screws, wedges and hydraulic rams all play the same game with different pieces. The screw is perhaps the most elegant of them, because it is nothing more than a ramp that has been wound around a cylinder, and a ramp is only a lever that has been stretched out flat.

Heat engines are where the accounting becomes genuinely subtle, and where a great many confident intuitions turn out to be wrong. Burning fuel releases energy readily enough, and the hot gas that results will push a piston or spin a turbine with real enthusiasm, but no arrangement of pistons, valves and clever linkages can turn all of that heat into motion. The reason is not that engineers have been insufficiently ingenious. It is a matter of principle. Heat is the disordered jostling of enormous numbers of particles, all moving in different directions at once, while useful work is orderly motion in one direction, and turning disorder into order always costs something. The most that any engine can extract depends on the difference between the temperature at which it takes heat in and the temperature at which it dumps the remainder, and since the cold side is usually the surrounding air or a river, and the hot side is limited by the melting point of whatever the engine is made of, the ceiling is lower than most people expect. A good power station may convert something like two fifths of the energy in its fuel into electricity, and a car engine considerably less than that. The rest leaves as warmth, up the chimney, out of the exhaust pipe, through the radiator. This is not waste in the sense of carelessness. It is the rent that the universe charges for the privilege of turning fire into movement.

Structures obey a quieter set of rules, but they are just as unforgiving. A beam laid across a gap sags in the middle, and when it sags the material along its upper surface is squeezed together while the material along its lower surface is stretched apart, which means that a single simple beam is doing two quite different jobs at once. Stone is superb at being squeezed and hopeless at being stretched, which is why stone beams must be short and fat, and why the arch was such an enormous advance. An arch is a curve arranged so that the load pushes every stone against its neighbours, converting what would have been a stretching force into a squeezing one, and thereby playing to the strength of the material rather than to its weakness. The price is that the arch pushes outwards at its feet as well as downwards, and that outward shove must be resisted by something massive, which is why old bridges have such heavy abutments and why cathedrals grew those famous external props. Steel and reinforced concrete changed the arithmetic again. Steel is happy in tension, concrete is happy in compression, and the trick of casting steel rods into concrete gives a composite that copes with both, provided that the rods are placed on the side that is being stretched. Put them on the wrong side and the structure will crack, slowly and then suddenly, in exactly the place where the designer's imagination failed.

Navigation is the branch of practical mathematics in which being wrong has the most immediate consequences, and its history is a long argument with the sky. Finding your latitude at sea has never been especially difficult, because the height of the sun above the horizon at noon, or the height of the pole star at night, depends in a straightforward way on how far north or south you have travelled, and a sextant that measures that angle to within a minute of arc will place you within a mile or so. Longitude was the hard problem, and it was hard for a reason that is worth stating plainly. The earth turns, so the sky sweeps overhead, and there is no fixed marker in it that tells you how far east or west you have gone. What longitude really measures is time. If you know the local time from the sun, and you also know the time at some reference place, then the difference between them is your longitude, at fifteen degrees for every hour. The whole problem therefore reduces to carrying an accurate clock through months of damp, heat, cold and violent motion, which is why the eventual solution came from the workshop of a clockmaker rather than from an observatory.

Before reliable clocks, and often afterwards as a check, sailors relied on dead reckoning, which is the disciplined art of guessing carefully. You know where you started. You know roughly what course you have steered, because the compass says so. You know roughly how fast you have gone, because a log line paid out over the stern told you so. Multiply speed by time, apply it along the course, and you have a new estimated position, which becomes the starting point for the next estimate. The weakness is obvious and unavoidable: every small error is carried forward and added to the next, so that uncertainty grows steadily with distance, like a rumour passed along a chain of people. Currents make it worse, because a current moves the whole ocean and the ship with it, silently, without registering on the log or the compass. Leeway makes it worse still, since a vessel sailing close to the wind slides sideways as well as forwards. Prudent navigators therefore aimed deliberately to one side of their destination, so that when land appeared they would know which way to turn along the coast, a technique that trades a longer voyage for a great deal less anxiety.

The magnetic compass, which made all of this possible, is itself a slightly treacherous instrument. It does not point to the geographic pole, about which the earth actually spins, but towards the magnetic pole, which is somewhere else entirely and which wanders from decade to decade. The difference between the two, called variation, changes with where you are as well as when you are, and charts must therefore print it and be reissued as it drifts. On top of that sits deviation, which is the error caused by the ship itself, since a vessel full of iron carries its own magnetic field around with it, and that field pulls the needle away from where it would otherwise settle. Deviation depends on which way the ship is heading, so it cannot be corrected once and forgotten. It must be measured on every heading, tabulated, and either compensated with small magnets bolted near the binnacle or allowed for in the arithmetic. A navigator who applies variation and deviation in the wrong direction will end up twice as far out as one who ignores both, which is a fine illustration of the general truth that a half remembered correction is more dangerous than none.

Satellite navigation replaced most of this, though it did not repeal any of the underlying geometry. A receiver in a phone or a car listens for signals from a constellation of satellites, each of which broadcasts, over and over, a message that amounts to a statement of who it is, where it is, and precisely when the message left. Because radio waves travel at a known speed, the delay between transmission and reception tells the receiver how far away that satellite was, and a known distance from a known point places you somewhere on the surface of a sphere. Two spheres intersect in a circle, three in a pair of points, one of which is absurd and can be discarded, and so three satellites would in principle be enough. In practice a fourth is needed, because the receiver's own clock is a cheap thing that cannot be trusted to the necessary accuracy, and the extra measurement lets it solve for the time error along with the position. The satellites carry atomic clocks instead, and those clocks must be corrected continually for effects that seem far too small to matter, including the fact that a clock in orbit runs at a very slightly different rate from one on the ground. Ignore those corrections and the whole system drifts into uselessness within a day.

Chemistry rewards the same insistence on mechanism. Ask why salt dissolves in water but not in oil, and the answer is not that water is a better solvent in some general and mysterious way. It is that a water molecule is bent rather than straight, with the oxygen at the apex holding electrons more tightly than the two hydrogens do, so that one end of the molecule carries a slight negative charge and the other end a slight positive one. Salt is built from charged particles stacked in a rigid lattice. When it meets water, the negative ends of the water molecules crowd around each positive ion and the positive ends crowd around each negative one, surrounding them, screening them from each other, and gradually coaxing them out of the crystal. Oil molecules have no such lopsidedness. They are long and greasy and electrically bland, and they have nothing to offer an ion that would compensate for leaving the comfort of the lattice, so the salt simply sits there. The same principle explains why grease will not wash off in cold water alone and why soap works, since a soap molecule is a deliberate compromise, greasy at one end and charged at the other, able to hold hands with both parties at once.

Acidity is another idea that becomes clear the moment it is stated in terms of particles rather than tastes. In any sample of water a tiny fraction of the molecules have come apart into a positively charged fragment and a negatively charged one, and the concentration of the positive fragment is what we mean by acidity. Because that concentration ranges over an enormous span, from fairly large in a strong acid to almost vanishingly small in a strong alkali, chemists express it as a logarithm, which compresses a range of many powers of ten into a comfortable scale running roughly from zero to fourteen. The consequence, often forgotten, is that each step on that scale represents a tenfold change. A solution two units lower than another is not slightly more acidic; it is a hundred times more so. Buffers are mixtures arranged so that adding acid or alkali barely moves the number at all, because a reservoir of one component soaks up whatever is added, and living things are full of them, since the chemistry of a cell would collapse if its acidity were allowed to swing about freely.

Rust is a slow fire. That statement sounds like a metaphor but it is very nearly a technical description, because both burning and rusting are reactions in which a substance combines with oxygen and releases energy, and the difference is one of speed rather than of kind. Iron rusts readily, and the rust that forms is flaky and porous, so it falls away and exposes fresh metal underneath, which is why an unprotected iron bar will eventually consume itself entirely. Aluminium reacts even more eagerly, yet aluminium window frames last for decades, and the reason is that the oxide it forms is dense and tightly bonded and clings to the surface as an invisible film a few millionths of a millimetre thick, sealing the metal beneath it. Painting steel, galvanising it with zinc, or bolting a lump of magnesium to a ship's hull are all ways of buying the same protection by other means. In the last case the sacrificial metal is chosen precisely because it corrodes more willingly than the steel does, so that as long as it is there it will be eaten instead.

Mathematics supplies the tools that make all of this expressible, and occasionally it supplies something more interesting, which is a result that could not have been guessed. Take the prime numbers, those whole numbers greater than one that cannot be split into a product of smaller whole numbers. They thin out as you go, which suggests they might eventually stop, and it is not obvious that they do not. The proof that they never stop is short enough to hold in the head. Suppose there were only finitely many primes. Multiply them all together and add one. The resulting number leaves a remainder of one when divided by any prime on the list, so no prime on the list divides it, yet every number greater than one has at least one prime divisor, so there must be a prime that was not on the list, and the assumption collapses. Nothing about that argument requires a computer, an experiment, or a grant. It requires only the willingness to suppose something false and follow it politely until it contradicts itself, which is a manoeuvre worth learning for its own sake.

Statistics is the branch that most often misleads people who have not been warned, and the average is the chief offender. An average is a single number standing in for a crowd, and it does that job well only when the crowd is reasonably tidy. If a village has ten households of modest means and one household of enormous wealth, the average income describes nobody at all, since it is far above what ten of the eleven actually receive. The middle value, found by lining everyone up and pointing at the person in the centre, would serve far better. Spread matters as much as centre, because two processes with identical averages and different variability behave completely differently in practice, and a bridge designed for the average load rather than the extreme one is a bridge waiting for a crowd. Then there is the persistent confusion between two things happening together and one causing the other. Ice cream sales and drownings rise in step, not because the first causes the second, but because hot weather causes both, and the number of such hidden third causes in any real dataset is larger than anyone would like.

Living things solve engineering problems too, and often by routes an engineer would not have chosen. Consider how a tall tree lifts water from its roots to leaves that may be sixty or more metres above them, without a pump and without any moving parts. There is no muscle involved and no valve. What happens instead is that water evaporates from the surfaces inside the leaves, and as each molecule leaves, the surface film it belonged to is pulled slightly tighter, and because water molecules cling to one another with surprising tenacity, that tug is transmitted down an unbroken thread of water through narrow pipes in the trunk all the way to the roots. The whole column is under tension, being pulled from above rather than pushed from below, which is why a tree that suffers an air bubble in one of those pipes loses the use of it permanently. The narrowness of the pipes is essential, since a thin thread of water can bear a pull that a wide one cannot, and the sheer number of them provides both redundancy and volume. On a hot day a mature tree may move several hundred litres this way, powered entirely by sunshine and the thirst of the air.

Animal navigation is stranger still, and less well understood. Birds that breed in the far north and winter thousands of miles south find their way with a combination of methods that vary between species and even between individuals. Some appear to use the position of the setting sun to fix a direction at dusk. Others learn the pattern of rotation of the night sky while still in the nest, identifying the point about which the stars appear to turn, which is a genuinely clever solution because it does not depend on recognising any particular star and therefore keeps working over the long slow wobble of the earth's axis. Many are sensitive to the magnetic field, and the mechanism seems to involve chemical reactions in the eye whose outcome depends faintly on the direction of that field, which would mean that some birds effectively see it as a pattern laid over the landscape. Young birds on a first migration often fly a fixed heading for a fixed period, an inherited instruction rather than a learned route, and only later acquire the ability to correct for being blown off course. Displace an experienced adult a thousand miles sideways and it will usually turn and compensate. Displace a juvenile and it will carry on doggedly along the heading it was born with.

Weather is the last and most public of these systems, and the one whose explanation most often gets truncated to the point of nonsense. Wind exists because the sun heats the earth unevenly, air over warm ground expands and rises, and the resulting differences in pressure make air flow from where there is more of it towards where there is less. So far the story is simple. What complicates it is that the earth is turning underneath the moving air, so that a parcel of air heading north from the tropics carries with it the greater eastward speed of the latitude it came from, and therefore drifts ahead of the ground beneath it. The effect is to bend moving air to the right in the northern half of the world and to the left in the southern, and it is why weather systems spiral rather than simply filling in, and why the wind near a depression blows almost along the lines of equal pressure instead of straight across them. Tides work by a related trick of frames. The moon pulls hardest on the near side of the earth, least on the far side, and by an intermediate amount on the middle, and it is the difference between those pulls, not their strength, that stretches the oceans into two bulges and gives most coasts two high tides a day. The sun does the same thing more weakly, and when the two line up the tides run large, and when they oppose each other the tides run small. Once you see that pattern you can predict it for years ahead. That is what an explanation is for.
